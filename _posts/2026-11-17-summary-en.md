### [vLLM v0.34.0 ships new models, default-on extensible KV cache, Model Runner V2](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/releases/tag/v0.34.0) ⭐️ 7.0/10

vLLM v0.34.0 was released on November 16, 2026, bundling 1,061 commits from 412 contributors \(120 new\). The release integrates 10 new models — including Mistral Large 4 \(1T total / 52B active parameters in FP8 and NVFP4\), MiniMax-M3.1-Flash, NVIDIA Nemotron-3.5-Transcribe, and Zyphra ZAYA1 — and makes the extensible KV cache the default, sizing the cache from memory actually free after CUDA graph capture and warmup rather than an estimate; the release notes report a larger cache \(e.g. MiniMax-M3 on H200: 433k → 672k tokens\) with higher throughput, though with a stated trade-off of 6–18% higher per-token decode latency \(TPOT\) on some H200 models at concurrency 128. Model Runner V1 is removed, making Model Runner V2 the only GPU runner, and the release notes claim speedups on Blackwell and MI355X hardware, including up to 1.95× decode for Step5 NVFP4 MoE on 4×GB200 and 1.24–1.40× faster long-prompt TTFT for GLM-5.3 on GB200. These performance figures come from the release notes and have not been independently reproduced. Several breaking changes apply: default block size moves from 16 to 64, ROCm 7.2 images are dropped in favor of ROCm 10.0, huggingface\_hub &gt;= 2.0.0 is required, and \`fp\_quant\` checkpoints and Marlin W4A8 quantization are removed.

github · khluu · Nov 16, 23:22

**「Background」** vLLM is a widely used open-source inference engine, and this release replaces two of its long-standing defaults. Previously, the KV cache was sized from a static \`gpu\_memory\_utilization\` estimate \(default 0.9\) rather than from memory actually free after warmup, and Model Runner V1 remained available alongside the V2 GPU runner behind the \`VLLM\_USE\_V2\_MODEL\_RUNNER\` flag. This version makes memory-measured extensible KV cache sizing the default and completes the V2 migration by removing V1 entirely, which is why several long-standing configuration options now fail or behave differently on upgrade.

**「Upgrade considerations」** Operators upgrading existing deployments must audit their launch configurations: \`VLLM\_USE\_V2\_MODEL\_RUNNER=0\` now fails at startup and V1 logits processors must be ported, the default \`--block-size\` change from 16 to 64 and the default-on extensible KV cache \(with \`gpu\_memory\_utilization\` now defaulting to 1.0\) can alter latency and memory behavior — pass \`--block-size 16\` and \`--gpu-memory-utilization 0.9\` or \`--no-enable-extensible-kv-cache\` to keep prior behavior. Additionally, ROCm 7.2 users must move to ROCm 10.0 wheels, and workloads relying on \`fp\_quant\` checkpoints or Marlin W4A8 quantization need migration paths \(Humming quantization for the latter\), so testing against the new defaults before rollout is advisable.

### [Google GTIG reports first observed fully automated AI-driven cyberattack pipeline](https://the-decoder.com/google-warns-that-fully-automated-cyberattacks-have-arrived-and-will-get-worse-fast/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Google&\#x27;s Threat Intelligence Group \(GTIG\) reports the first observed instance of a cyberattack pipeline that fully automates vulnerability discovery and exploitation with no human in the loop. The finding comes from an exposed server running an AI framework called &quot;exploit\_agent&quot; connected to multiple language models; the system compares software code before and after patches to identify vulnerabilities, develops exploits, and launches them automatically. Russian-language comments in the configuration files suggest the operator speaks Russian, and server data indicates targeting of French websites in media, entertainment, and education. GTIG calls this a critical turning point but notes the attacks currently trade stealth for speed, leaving forensic traces that make them easier to detect.

rss · The Decoder · Nov 16, 19:42

**「Background」** AI-assisted cyber operations have been documented in stages: first attackers used large language models to help write phishing content and malware code with human oversight, and later semi-automated workflows where AI tools supported reconnaissance or exploit development while people made the key decisions. Google&\#x27;s Threat Intelligence Group \(GTIG\) has tracked AI-enabled threat activity through its AI Threat Tracker, which monitors how adversaries incorporate generative AI into their operations. The newly reported finding marks a shift in that progression: rather than AI assisting human operators, an exposed server running a framework called &quot;exploit\_agent&quot; linked multiple language models into a pipeline that compared pre- and post-patch code to find vulnerabilities, generate exploits, and run them on its own.

**「Why it matters」** Organizations deploying internet-facing software should treat patch-diffing as an attack vector: the observed tooling derives exploits directly from patched code, shortening the window between a patch&\#x27;s release and its exploitation and raising the priority of rapid, verified patch deployment. GTIG&\#x27;s finding that these pipelines are fast but forensically noisy also means current detection and incident-response practices remain effective, but that advantage may erode as the tooling matures.

### [Optimizely Snippet Pipeline Compromised to Serve Malware on Major Sites](https://blog.report-uri.com/worlds-largest-brands-infected-with-malware-via-optimizely/) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Attackers injected malicious code into Optimizely&\#x27;s snippet pipeline, causing major websites including CNN and BBC that embed Optimizely&\#x27;s experimentation script to serve malware to visitors. Optimizely&\#x27;s own sites were affected too, with snippets on www.optimizely.com and login.optimizely.com carrying the code. Optimizely&\#x27;s CDN served exactly what it was given, so this was not a CDN cache-poisoning attack; the root cause — how the attacker obtained write access across many unrelated customer projects — remains publicly unexplained.

hackernews · kristjansson · Nov 16, 23:00 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50524767)

**「Background」** Optimizely sells A/B testing and experimentation software that major publishers integrate by embedding a small JavaScript snippet on their pages, which the vendor&\#x27;s CDN then delivers and updates centrally. That architecture means a change on the vendor&\#x27;s side propagates instantly to every customer site at once, so a compromise of the vendor&\#x27;s snippet pipeline can inject malware into high-traffic websites like CNN and BBC without any action by the sites themselves.

**「Practical consequences」** Organizations relying on third-party tag and experimentation scripts face direct exposure: the compromise reportedly reached the BBC, CNN, HSBC, Equifax, Zoom, and over 170 other brands through Optimizely&\#x27;s snippet pipeline, meaning even their own login pages could carry attacker-controlled JavaScript. Security teams should audit vendor-injected scripts, restrict or sandbox third-party tags \(for example with a strict CSP or tools like NoScript for high-risk browsing\), and require vendors to demonstrate release controls such as phased rollouts and automated security checks before trusting production snippets.

**「What engineers are saying」** Commenters questioned why high-traffic sites like CNN and BBC handle sensitive actions on the same pages that load third-party scripts, with skybrian noting a missing step in how the malware would actually exploit visitors. Others blamed the tag-management model itself: stickfigure argued Google Tag Manager&\#x27;s promise of letting marketing add tags without engineering is how news sites ended up shipping 10MB of JavaScript per article, and mcoliver called for accountability mechanisms such as phased rollouts and automatic security checks at both vendors and large publishers.

### [US Moves to Cut China&\#x27;s Remote Access to AI Chips Abroad](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2026/11/us-moves-to-cut-chinas-remote-access-to-ai-chips/) ⭐️ 8.0/10

The US Commerce Department is preparing an interim rule that closes a loophole letting companies in China and other D:5 embargoed countries \(including Russia, Iran, and North Korea\) remotely access restricted AI chips via data centers in Southeast Asia and Brazil. Under the rule, exporters must obtain licenses to ship AI chips and servers to data centers anywhere in the world if those centers may provide compute to restricted-country companies, with the threshold set at data centers supplying over 12 million TPP—roughly the power of about 750 Nvidia H100 or H200 chips. The rule also adds licensing requirements for chip exports to Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, Thailand, and Brazil \(waived for US-based companies\), covers indirect access through brokers, requires operators to vet customer ownership, gives existing data center operators 90 days to obtain licenses, and formally rescinds the Biden administration&\#x27;s January 2025 AI Diffusion Rule. Commercial recommendation systems, such as those in e-commerce and social media, are exempt.

rss · Ars Technica · Nov 16, 19:10

**「Background」** The US already requires chipmakers to obtain licenses before shipping advanced AI chips to companies in embargoed countries, including China, Russia, and Iran, but firms in those countries could still access such hardware by renting compute from data centers abroad. The new interim rule extends those controls to data centers worldwide that could serve restricted parties, and adds licensing requirements for exports to Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, Thailand, and Brazil, which the Commerce Department identified as carrying a higher-than-normal risk of chip diversion.

**「Concrete impact」** Data center operators in Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, and Brazil that serve restricted-country customers must obtain US export licenses within 90 days of the rule taking effect, and exporters must monitor customers&\#x27; ownership structures to detect indirect access through brokers. Operators and cloud providers serving Chinese or other D:5-country clients should review whether their deployments exceed the ~12 million TPP threshold \(roughly 750 Nvidia H100/H200 chips\) and prepare license applications; chips destined for US-headquartered companies in the five listed countries are exempt, though the rule&\#x27;s interaction with commercial recommendation systems also leaves room for case-by-case interpretation.
