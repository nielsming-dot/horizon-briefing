Stanford researchers Owen Queen and James Zou tested 50 widely cited claims about large language models against 20 models released between July 2024 and September 2026 by eight developers and found that 44 percent have &quot;expired,&quot; meaning the original effect has largely disappeared. Among the 22 findings with an estimable expiration date, the average lifespan was 15.7 months; after one year 16 percent had expired, rising to 39 percent after two years, while another 34 percent of claims weakened significantly, 16 percent still held, and 6 percent got stronger. Negative-capability claims decayed fastest: five of nine reasoning-related findings are expired and none held up, including the &quot;strawberry&quot; letter-counting failure, arithmetic in unfamiliar number systems, lost information in long-context middles, and abandonment of correct answers under user pushback. In a citation-fabrication case study, GPT-3.5&\#x27;s 55 percent hallucinated-reference rate fell to 0 percent for GPT-6.1 Sol and 1 percent for Claude Sonnet 5.5, but Qwen3.7-Flash, a 2026 model, still fabricated 69 percent.

**「Background」** Empirical LLM research has long reported model weaknesses as if they were stable properties, with well-known examples like the &\#x27;Reversal Curse,&\#x27; letter-counting failures, and citation hallucination becoming standard references in the literature. Prior work such as the 2023 Walters and Wilder study on fabricated scientific references helped establish these findings, but such papers rarely re-test claims against newer model generations. ### [StateBench: A Four-Level Benchmark for Long-Context State Tracking](https://huggingface.co/blog/allenai/statebench) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Hugging Face and AllenAI introduced StateBench, an open benchmark with a published tech report, data, and code that measures how well language models track evolving entity state across long documents. The benchmark defines four difficulty levels of state tracking—r-trivial \(immutable assignment\), aperiodic \(reassignment by value\), soluble \(self-referential updates\), and general finite \(assignment by reference, e.g., the S₅ cup-swap problem\)—grounded in Krohn–Rhodes theory, with matched problems across levels so only the assignment type changes. Testing open pretrained models \(Olmo 3 and Olmo Hybrid variants from 275M to 7B parameters, all pretrained on the same data with no contexts beyond 64,000 tokens\), the authors found most models did well on r-trivial, degraded on aperiodic as inputs lengthened, and stayed at chance on general finite problems. Frontier models \(Gemini 3.8 Flash, Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-6 Sol and Luna\) handled the two easier levels but dropped to 13% accuracy or lower on the longest general finite problems; in targeted training experiments with 275M-parameter models trained on sequences up to 256 assignments and tested up to 64,000, Gated DeltaNet and hybrid models generalized nearly perfectly on r-trivial, aperiodic, and soluble problems while transformers degraded and never learned general finite problems. These are the authors&\#x27; reported results from the initial release; independent community validation has not yet been demonstrated.

rss · Hugging Face Blog · Nov 3, 17:15

**「Background」** State tracking has known theoretical limits in transformers: prior work has shown they struggle with problems requiring chains of dependent updates over long inputs, such as the S5 ball-swapping task, which motivated recurrent and hybrid architectures like Gated DeltaNet that maintain a running memory. Earlier state-tracking evaluations tended to focus on these hardest cases, leaving a gap in measuring whether architectures differ on simpler, more common forms of state tracking—such as merely recovering the latest value of a variable. StateBench builds on Krohn–Rhodes theory, an algebraic framework that decomposes state-tracking problems into atomic primitives, to define a four-level difficulty hierarchy spanning these cases.

**「Practical relevance for model builders」** Teams evaluating long-context models for code comprehension or multi-step document reasoning should note that strong performance on retrieval-style benchmarks may not reflect ability on chained state updates: StateBench&\#x27;s results show frontier models failing on harder levels as inputs grow, and the benchmark&\#x27;s finding that format matters \(275M hybrid models scored near ceiling on RULER-style r-trivial problems but near chance on identical problems framed as spreadsheet edits\) suggests evaluations should test the same problem in multiple formats. Because data and code are released, the benchmark can be adopted directly, though its four levels are synthetic variable-assignment problems rather than natural documents.

The book is a reference resource rather than news of a release or capability.

hackernews · ibobev · Nov 3, 21:51 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50358702)

**「Background」** The book, maintained by InterDB author Takahiro Ito \(interdb.jp\), walks through PostgreSQL&\#x27;s core mechanisms—MVCC, buffer management, and so on—by reading the source code, and it has long been a standard free reference for engineers who want more than documentation-level knowledge. Two concepts underpin the chapters readers highlight: PostgreSQL&\#x27;s MVCC model, which keeps old row versions as new tuples rather than overwriting them, and HOT \(Heap-Only Tuple\) updates, an optimization that avoids index maintenance when an updated row&\#x27;s indexed columns are unchanged and the new version fits on the same page. Understanding how MVCC leaves dead tuples behind is what makes the HOT discussion—and tuning knobs like fillfactor—meaningful.

**「Practical takeaways」** Readers report concrete operational lessons from the HOT update chapter: HOT updates only occur when no indexed column changes and the new tuple fits on the same page, meaning an index on a frequently updated column \(such as status or updated\_at\) disables HOT for updates to that table. Lowering fillfactor to around 80-90 on update-heavy tables leaves page space for HOT updates, and comparing n\_tup\_hot\_upd to n\_tup\_upd in pg\_stat\_user\_tables offers a cheap way to check how often HOT is actually happening.

**「Reader discussion」** Commenter gopalv said recent interest in PostgreSQL&\#x27;s serializable snapshot isolation \(SSI\) implementation was sparked by the latest Jepsen report on particledb, and recommended reading the HOT update section plus the zHeap and TOAST notes to build a mental model of what Postgres does and does not do. Commenter getbrew agreed the HOT chapter has the most practical value, citing the indexing and fillfactor details above.

### [Anthropic unveils open-source FlashProve for cryptographically verifying AI model outputs](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/03/anthropic-is-working-on-a-system-to-prove-ai-models-identity/) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Anthropic researchers announced FlashProve, an open-source cryptographic system designed to provide &quot;provable inference&quot; — mathematical proof that a given AI output genuinely came from a specific model. The system works by having labs publish a &quot;fingerprint&quot; for each model that can be matched to outputs and verified without revealing the model&\#x27;s private weights, and it is designed to be lightweight enough to verify locally. It is intended to guard against model substitution, including by the serving lab itself — for example, secretly swapping in a cheaper model to cut costs or a more expensive one to inflate benchmark results — and to let users verify third-party claims that a specific advanced model performed a task. A technical specification and working code for parts of the system are available on GitHub under an open-source license, but the researchers describe this as intermediate research progress with no plans to deploy it at Anthropic or any other lab.

rss · TechCrunch · Nov 3, 22:41

**「What provable inference is」** Provable inference refers to cryptographic techniques that produce a mathematical proof accompanying an AI output, showing it was generated by a specific model rather than merely asserting so. This matters because most commercial models are served behind APIs, and users currently have no way to verify the lab is running the model it claims — a gap that allows cheaper models to be silently substituted or benchmark results to be inflated. A key technical obstacle has been that verification schemes often require access to a model&\#x27;s weights, which closed-model developers like Anthropic and OpenAI keep secret, making fingerprint-based approaches that protect the weights notable.

**「Why it matters」** Until such a system is actually deployed, users currently have no cryptographic way to confirm which model produced an output, so FlashProve&\#x27;s practical effect depends on AI labs choosing to publish fingerprints — something no lab is yet committed to. Developers and evaluators can already examine the specification and code on GitHub and contribute improvements, which the researchers explicitly invite.
