### [Retatrutide Cuts Knee OA Pain in Obesity in Phase 3 TRIUMPH-4](https://www.medpagetoday.com/meetingcoverage/acr/123776) ⭐️ 8.0/10

In the phase 3 TRIUMPH-4 trial, the investigational triple-agonist retatrutide significantly reduced knee osteoarthritis pain and improved physical function in 445 patients with obesity and knee OA. Over 68 weeks, WOMAC pain scores fell by 4.48 points with 9 mg and 4.41 points with 12 mg weekly injections versus 2.43 points with placebo, and roughly 70% of retatrutide-treated patients achieved 70% pain improvement versus 26% on placebo; WOMAC function improved by about 4.2 points versus 2.06 with placebo. Participants lost more than 25% of body weight with retatrutide, without an obvious plateau, alongside reduced high-sensitivity CRP and improved lipids. Findings were presented at the ACR annual meeting and published simultaneously in NEJM; adverse-event discontinuations were 13% and 18% with the two doses versus 4% with placebo, and retatrutide remains investigational.

rss · MedPage Today · Nov 8, 20:37

**「Why It Matters」** Knee osteoarthritis is a common cause of pain and disability, and excess body weight is a well-recognized driver of symptom severity, which is why weight loss has long been recommended alongside exercise and analgesics, and why the ACR&\#x27;s newly released osteoarthritis guidelines place increased emphasis on weight management. Retatrutide is an investigational triple agonist of the GLP-1, GIP, and glucagon receptors, and prior trials have shown greater weight loss with it than with single-action GLP-1 drugs like semaglutide or the dual agonist tirzepatide, though those agents have not yet been established as disease-modifying osteoarthritis therapies. TRIUMPH-4 extends this line of work by testing directly whether such weight loss translates into meaningful knee pain and disability reduction in patients with obesity.

**「Impact」** If approved, retatrutide could add a pharmacologic weight-loss option to knee OA management in patients with obesity, complementing new ACR guidance that emphasizes weight loss; for now, the results support further trials of disease modification rather than an immediate change in practice, particularly given the notable side-effect burden and unanswered questions about metabolically normal OA patients.

A randomized controlled trial published in the New England Journal of Medicine and presented at the American Heart Association&\#x27;s scientific sessions found that a multifaceted community and EMS training strategy did not improve survival with favorable neurologic outcome after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. Starting in 2021, researchers randomly assigned 60 North Carolina counties \(about 8.5 million residents\) to intensive training of 911 dispatchers, first responders, and community members, or to standard practice; over five years, 22,459 adults \(median age 64, 37% women, 33% Black, 29% rural\) suffered cardiac arrest — 11,615 in intervention counties and 10,844 in control counties. Survival remained low and unchanged despite the intervention, contradicting earlier observational hints. However, an exploratory analysis in the trial&\#x27;s fourth and final year showed an increase in bystander CPR, suggesting effects may require longer or more intense intervention.

rss · STAT News · Nov 8, 22:15

**「Why this matters」** Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest strikes about 350,000 people in the United States each year, and only about 1 in 10 survives, with survival odds falling roughly 10% for every minute without CPR. Because immediate bystander CPR and defibrillation are the main determinants of outcome, public-health strategies such as dispatcher-assisted CPR, first-responder AED training, and mass community education have been widely promoted, and earlier observational studies — including experience from Denmark, where about 80% of the population is CPR-trained and survival reaches roughly 16% — suggested such comprehensive training improves survival. The new randomized trial tested that assumption directly and found no improvement in survival with favorable neurologic outcome, although an exploratory analysis in the trial&\#x27;s final year showed more bystander CPR being delivered.

**「Implications for resuscitation policy」** The null result challenges the assumption, drawn from observational studies, that broad community CPR education alone will translate into more lives saved, and it cautions against committing large-scale public-health resources to short-duration training campaigns without evidence of survival benefit. The trial trained roughly 5% of the covered population, far below Denmark&\#x27;s roughly 80% training rate, where bystander CPR, defibrillation \(11% vs 2% in the U.S.\), and survival \(about 16% vs 10%\) are all substantially higher; the study team is following the North Carolina population for at least another year to determine whether delayed effects on survival emerge.

The pseudogap is an unexplained regime above the superconducting transition temperature in which measurements find far fewer electrons than expected at energies where they should be present. The team reports that noise measurements offer new information about this state, which has resisted explanation since cuprates&\#x27; high-temperature superconductivity was discovered in the late 1980s. The source coverage provides qualitative description rather than specific measurements, uncertainties, or constraints, so the precise scope of the new findings cannot be assessed from the article alone.

rss · Ars Technica Science · Nov 8, 12:15

**「Background」** Cuprates, copper-oxide materials discovered to superconduct at liquid-nitrogen temperatures in the late 1980s, remain unexplained nearly 40 years later, unlike conventional superconductors that work only a few degrees above absolute zero. A key obstacle is the pseudogap: above the superconducting transition temperature, spectroscopic probes of many kinds find far fewer electrons than expected at energies where they should be present, and no consensus exists on why. The new Science study, led by Milan Allan of Ludwig Maximilian University of Munich and Ingmar Swart of Utrecht University, applies electrical noise spectroscopy—measuring faint current fluctuations in a cuprate—as a fresh probe of this state.

**「Why it matters」** Because the pseudogap&\#x27;s origin is a central unsolved question for explaining how cuprates superconduct at liquid-nitrogen temperatures, an independent experimental probe like noise spectroscopy constrains competing theoretical models of the state and complements established techniques that have observed the missing electrons but not explained them.

Using the James Webb Space Telescope, astronomers identified 104 gravitationally magnified stellar events in the Dragon arc, a lensed galaxy roughly 6.5 billion light-years away. This more than doubles the previous record of 44 such detections in the same galaxy. Because magnified stellar events expose some of the most luminous stars and can indicate the presence of dark matter, the dataset offers a rare view of the galaxy&\#x27;s stellar population and a possible hint at the nature of dark matter.

rss · Phys.org Space · Nov 8, 19:20

**「Why the Dragon galaxy is a stellar magnification laboratory」** The Dragon galaxy sits behind a massive foreground galaxy cluster whose gravity bends and amplifies its light through gravitational lensing, allowing individual transient stellar events — such as luminous stars brightening — to be resolved at a distance of about 6.5 billion light-years. When compact objects in the foreground cluster add short-lived microlensing boosts on top of this magnification, each stellar event can also constrain how much of the cluster&\#x27;s mass is in small, dim structures, which is why such events are proposed as a possible probe of dark matter. Before this JWST program, 44 such events had been detected in the same galaxy, making the new dataset of 104 events the largest census of magnified stellar events in a single lensed galaxy to date.

**「Impact」** With 104 events cataloged in a single lensed galaxy, future observing programs can use this enlarged sample to constrain microlensing-based probes of dark matter and to characterize the luminous stellar population that gravitational lensing makes individually detectable at cosmological distance.

### [Rubin Observatory spots steeply inclined retrograde object R631 beyond Neptune](https://earthsky.org/space/rubin-retrograde-object-far-solar-system-r631/) ⭐️ 6.0/10

The Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile has identified an object beyond Neptune, temporarily designated R631, with an orbital inclination of 142 degrees — meaning it orbits retrograde, opposite to the direction of most planets. The object appears over four months of Rubin data from December 2025 through March 2026, currently sits about 32 AU from the sun, and, if the preliminary orbit holds, takes roughly 547.84 Earth-years per orbit with a perihelion of 24 AU on October 31, 2047. Astronomer Marshall Eubanks suggested on the Minor Planet Mailing List that R631 could be about 62 miles \(100 km\) across and comparable in nature to giant Comet Bernardinelli–Bernstein. The designation is temporary and the discovery has not yet been peer-reviewed; R631 awaits an official designation from the Minor Planet Center.

rss · EarthSky · Nov 8, 12:03

**「What retrograde motion means」** Most bodies in the solar system orbit the sun in the same counterclockwise direction in a roughly flat plane, a remnant of the spinning gas and dust cloud from which the sun and planets formed; in astronomical usage, any orbit inclined more than 90 degrees to that plane is classed as retrograde. Retrograde objects are rare, and highly inclined retrograde orbits are best known among comets, such as the Kreutz sungrazer family, whose members — including the Great Comet of 1882 — share a 142-degree-inclined orbit like the one reported for R631. Such orbits are generally explained either by gravitational scattering off a giant planet such as Neptune or a passing star, or, more speculatively, by capture from outside the solar system.

**「What to watch」** If R631 proves cometary, it could show outgassing activity as it approaches its 2047 perihelion at 24 AU, as Comet Bernardinelli–Bernstein did when it produced jets at 16.6 AU. Follow-up observations and official orbit confirmation by the Minor Planet Center will determine whether the 142-degree retrograde orbit and possible ~100 km size hold up.
