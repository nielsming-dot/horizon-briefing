A METR audit conducted from August 12 to October 29, 2026, with unprecedented access to Anthropic&\#x27;s internal data found that the lab&\#x27;s model improvement rate roughly doubled since October 2025, from about 15 to 29 points per year on METR&\#x27;s AECI composite benchmark. METR estimates roughly 80 percent of that acceleration comes from AI being used in Anthropic&\#x27;s own research, with the uncertainty range spanning an acceleration factor of 1.3 to 3, and finds no bottlenecks likely to slow the pace over the next twelve months. Anthropic&\#x27;s own internal analysis implies an acceleration factor of 1.97, and the company itself calls it plausible that the Responsible Scaling Policy warning threshold—triggered when AI-driven automation doubles the pace of progress—was already crossed around July 15, 2026, even though its public August 2026 risk report stated the acceleration was less than a factor of 2. Anthropic retained unilateral redaction rights and removed most of the evidence METR rated most informative, including forecasts for future models and the bottleneck analysis, meaning the public must rely heavily on METR&\#x27;s own judgment; the organization also canceled a planned audit of Anthropic&\#x27;s compute allocation between capability and safety research.

rss · The Decoder · Nov 6, 13:40

**「Background」** Anthropic&\#x27;s Responsible Scaling Policy defines AI-driven acceleration of its own research pace—specifically a doubling of model improvement attributable to automated AI research—as a warning-level risk indicator, on the theory that such a feedback loop could produce sudden, hard-to-control capability jumps. METR is a nonprofit research organization that studies whether AI developers could lose control of their systems, and it had recently been named by Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei as part of a push to bring external auditors into the company. **「Impact」** For organizations tracking frontier-lab risk commitments, the case shows an external audit can produce a materially different picture than a lab&\#x27;s public risk report—here, an internal 1.97 acceleration factor against a public claim of less than 2—while unilateral redaction rights leave auditors&\#x27; conclusions largely unverifiable. ### [Sodium-ion batteries near cost parity with LFP, 70 GWh under supply agreements](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2026/11/06/sodium-ion-batteries-approach-cost-parity-with-lfp-with-70-gwh-covered-by-supply-agreements/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Sodium-ion batteries are approaching cost parity with lithium iron phosphate \(LFP\), and 70 GWh of sodium-ion capacity is already covered by supply agreements, according to pv magazine. Most of this committed capacity is destined for stationary storage systems. CATL is only now ramping up production of its Naxtra sodium-ion cells, so the cost convergence is arriving early in the chemistry&\#x27;s commercial ramp.

hackernews · doener · Nov 6, 23:00 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50399657)

**「Background」** Lithium iron phosphate \(LFP\) is currently the dominant low-cost battery chemistry, widely used in electric vehicles and stationary energy storage, and serves as the benchmark that newer chemistries must match on price. Sodium-ion batteries use abundant sodium instead of lithium, trading lower energy density for potential cost, safety, and cold-weather advantages; large-scale production, such as CATL&\#x27;s Naxtra cells, is only now ramping up, which is why the reported 70 GWh of supply agreements signals the technology moving from pilot stage toward commercial volume.

**「Why it matters」** Stationary storage buyers get an increasingly price-competitive alternative to lithium-based chemistry, and each GWh of sodium-ion supply offsets lithium demand, which could relieve pressure on higher-value battery chemistries. Notably, some sodium-ion products are already purchasable today: OHMMU sells 12V sodium batteries as lead-acid replacements and has extended its warranty on them to three years, up from one.

**「Community reaction」** Commenters largely welcomed the development: syntaxing said they are eager for a sodium-based home battery because of safety and low-temperature operation, accepting lower energy density for stationary use, and hbarka reported positive first-hand experience with sodium 12V batteries in an EV. Others pushed back on the hype: mateusfreira complained that announcements appear daily without products consumers can actually buy.
