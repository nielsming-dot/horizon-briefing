**「Background」** Protein design has historically been the domain of specialized AI systems: geometric or diffusion-based models propose 3D backbone structures, and sequence-design models such as the MPNN family then produce amino-acid sequences predicted to fold into those structures, with additional specialized models filtering candidates for foldability and binding. Because a protein&\#x27;s amino-acid sequence largely determines its structure, commenters on the post argue that this sequence-generation step is where the substantive design work lies, which is why OpenAI&\#x27;s framing of GPT-6.2 as having &quot;intrinsic&quot; protein-design capability is contested: the post itself states that a specialized model, SolubleMPNN, was used to turn GPT-6.2&\#x27;s geometric designs into testable sequences. The claim, if it holds up, would suggest a shift in which general-purpose code-writing LLMs act as the generative core of a scientific workflow, with specialized models relegated to validation and sequence realization.

**「Impact」** For teams building AI-for-science pipelines, the workflow described here suggests a practical pattern: general-purpose LLMs orchestrating and writing code around specialized domain models like SolubleMPNN, rather than replacing them. Anyone evaluating similar claims should check whether sequence generation and experimental validation were handled by specialized models or lab controls, since those steps determine whether the general model&\#x27;s contribution is substantive or architectural.

**「Why the matrix multiplication exponent matters」** Matrix multiplication is a foundational operation in machine learning and scientific computing, and for decades researchers have sought the exact exponent ω such that two n×n matrices can be multiplied in O\(n^ω\) time. The standard algorithm takes n³ steps, while Strassen&\#x27;s 1969 algorithm showed the exponent can be below 3, and successive improvements pushed upper bounds toward 2, with a long-standing conjecture that ω equals exactly 2. According to community discussion, an O\(n^2.25\) algorithm was already known before this preprint, so the new contribution is proving the matching lower bound of Ω\(n^2.25\), pinning the exponent at Θ\(n^2.25\).

**「Why it matters」** If verified, the lower bound Ω\(n^2.25\) closes decades of debate about whether matrix multiplication can approach n^2, setting a firm limit for algorithm designers in scientific computing and the linear-algebra kernels underlying machine learning. ### [Attackers forge Check Point admin cookies by reconstructing V8 PRNG state](https://www.greynoise.io/blog/check-point-security-management-servers-intrusion-campaign) ⭐️ 8.0/10

GreyNoise reports an active intrusion campaign targeting Check Point security management servers, in which attackers exploit an HFS login endpoint that issues signed session cookies with &quot;sid&quot; values generated by JavaScript&\#x27;s Math.random\(\). By performing several logins in succession, the actor collected enough PRNG output to reconstruct the internal state of Node.js&\#x27;s V8 PRNG algorithm, recover the HFS session-cookie signing key, and forge a signed admin session cookie. According to GreyNoise&\#x27;s timeline, the exploitation activity was observed on 2026-10-02 at 13:17:45 UTC.

hackernews · louiereederson · Nov 24, 16:32 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50630594)

**「Why Math.random\(\) session IDs can be reverse-engineered」** V8&\#x27;s Math.random\(\) uses a pseudo-random number generator that is fast but not cryptographically secure: once an observer collects enough successive outputs, the generator&\#x27;s internal state can be reconstructed and future \(and past\) outputs predicted. Because the targeted servers drew session identifiers \(&quot;sid&quot; values\) from Math.random\(\) and signed cookies with a key derived through that path, repeated login requests gave the attacker enough samples to recover the cookie-signing key and mint valid admin session cookies. This continues a long line of real-world exploits against non-cryptographic PRNGs used for security purposes, which is why commenters greeted the technique as an old problem resurfacing in a new target.

**「Impact」** Organizations running Check Point security management servers, especially those exposed to the internet, face a demonstrated technique for forged admin access; a commenter questioned whether affected customers had these servers reachable from the internet and suggested CVE-2026-16232 as the possible initial vector, which remains unclear in the reporting. Administrators should verify their management servers are not directly internet-facing and confirm they are patched.

**「Community reaction」** Commenters focused on the PRNG technique, with one arguing that Math.random\(\) itself should be made secure rather than leaving hundreds of applications to misuse it, while another warned that LLM-driven attack automation could soon put such capabilities in the hands of mass numbers of criminals. The precise initial access vector remains a point of uncertainty in the discussion.
