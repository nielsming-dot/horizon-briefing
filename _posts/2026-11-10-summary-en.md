### [Infineon demonstrates world&\#x27;s first 300mm silicon carbide power wafer](https://www.infineon.com/press-release/2026/infpr202611-028) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Infineon announced on November 9, 2026 what it calls the world&\#x27;s first functional power devices processed on 300-millimeter silicon carbide \(SiC\) wafers, an industry first for wide-bandgap power semiconductor manufacturing. The company states that demonstrating feasibility of the 300mm SiC process lays the foundation for larger-scale production, though this is a corporate announcement of a milestone rather than an independently verified or shipped mass-production capability. 300mm wafers offer roughly twice the usable area per wafer compared with the prevailing smaller SiC formats, which would reduce per-chip cost if the process can be industrialized. No mass-production timelines were disclosed.

hackernews · sshah03 · Nov 9, 18:25 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50429430)

**「Background: Why wafer size matters in silicon carbide」** Silicon carbide is a wide-bandgap semiconductor used in power devices such as electric-vehicle inverters and fast chargers, where it handles higher voltages and temperatures than ordinary silicon. SiC wafers are harder to grow and process than silicon — the crystals must be grown as boules and sliced into wafers — and the industry has historically worked with smaller diameters than the 300-millimeter wafers that became standard for silicon decades ago. Because chip cost falls as more dies fit on each wafer, moving SiC to 300 millimeters is a long-pursued route to cheaper power devices, and Infineon says it reuses existing 300-millimeter silicon equipment for the process.

**「What this means for power semiconductor customers」** For SiC wafer buyers, Infineon&\#x27;s announcement of functional power devices on 300mm wafers signals that cost-per-die reductions could eventually reach power products, since 300mm wafers yield roughly twice the usable area of 200mm. The move is not unique in headline terms: Wolfspeed announced its own 300mm SiC technology breakthrough in January 2026, though Infineon&\#x27;s claim centers specifically on processing functional devices, not just producing wafers. Customers evaluating SiC suppliers should treat both announcements as early feasibility milestones rather than shipping capacity, and watch for qualification and volume timelines before expecting pricing benefits.

**「Community discussion」** Commenters questioned why the transition to 300mm took so long given that existing 300mm silicon equipment is reusable, and one user raised whether Wolfspeed had already reached 300mm SiC earlier in the year, suggesting the &quot;industry first&quot; claim may be contested. Others wondered about Infineon&\#x27;s unrelated AI hardware strategy, while one commenter proposed reviving European DRAM production.

### [Show HN: Watched.earth Finds Every Satellite That Photographed Any Location in the Past Week](https://www.watched.earth/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Developer a585gupt released watched.earth, an open-source browser tool that lists every satellite that passed over a user-entered location in the last 7 days and links the real Sentinel-2, Landsat, and NASA images from those passes. It propagates orbits with SGP4 using the CelesTrak catalog entirely in-browser in about half a second, renders all ~17,000 tracked objects on the GPU by solving Kepler&\#x27;s equation each frame, and marks a location as &quot;watched&quot; only when an actual image exists. The author reports the pass search was verified against brute-force checks at the poles, antimeridian, and small swaths with zero missed passes, and that the code is available on GitHub.

hackernews · a585gupt · Nov 9, 17:51 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50428907)

**「Where the data comes from」** Sentinel-2 \(ESA/Copernicus\) and Landsat are public Earth-observation programs whose imagery is freely downloadable, which is what lets the tool show an actual photo rather than just a predicted flyover. The pass predictions come from SGP4, the standard orbital-propagation algorithm, run against CelesTrak&\#x27;s public catalog of orbit data, so all computation can happen locally in the browser without a server.

**「Why it matters」** Users can quickly find out whether imagery of a specific place already exists without navigating Copernicus or similar portals; a commenter who works in geospatial noted the site makes it easy to see the exact image a satellite took, and another said it is more convenient than Copernicus for planning backpacking trips. The site does not store user locations, per the author.

**「Community reaction」** Commenters on Hacker News praised the project&\#x27;s execution and integration of multiple data sources, with geospatial professionals highlighting the ability to view the exact image captured by a satellite. One comment speculated that Claude may have written much of the code but argued that knowing what to ask for was itself impressive; the author has not confirmed or denied this.
